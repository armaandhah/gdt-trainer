01

What an engineering drawing is for

A CAD model describes a perfect part. No real part is perfect. Every hole a machinist drills comes out a little big or a little small, a little left or a little right. The drawing answers the question the CAD model can't: how far off is still OK?

That makes a drawing a contract between three people:

  • The designer says what the part must do and how much variation it can take.
  • The machinist picks processes that will land inside those limits.
  • The inspector measures the finished part and accepts or rejects it based only on what the drawing says.

If it isn't on the drawing, nobody is obligated to meet it. If the drawing is ambiguous, two people can measure the same part and disagree about whether it's good.

Example

You design a bracket with a 10 mm hole for a bolt. The shop makes 50 of them. Real measurements: 10.03, 9.98, 10.11, 10.01, and so on. Which ones are good? Without a tolerance on the drawing, there is no answer. With 10 ±0.1, the answer is clear: 9.9 to 10.1 passes, so the 10.11 part fails.

Common beginner mistake

Putting every number on the drawing "just in case." Each dimension is a requirement someone has to make and check. Dimension what matters for function, once, from the right place.

Same design, three real parts. The drawing's tolerance decides which ones ship.

02

Anatomy of a drawing sheet

Almost every drawing you'll ever see has the same five regions. Learn where they are and you can find your way around any sheet.

  • 1Border and zones. Letters down the side, numbers across the top, like a map grid. "See zone B3" tells someone exactly where to look on a big sheet.
  • 2Views. Flat pictures of the part from different directions, with dimensions. This is most of the sheet. Lesson 03 covers how views work.
  • 3Notes. Requirements that apply to the whole part or are easier written as text: material spec, finish, "break all sharp edges", heat treat.
  • 4Title block. Part name and number, material, units, scale, who drew and checked it, and the general tolerances that apply to any dimension without its own tolerance.
  • 5Revision block. A log of changes. Rev A, Rev B, and so on. Always make sure you're looking at the latest rev before you make or buy anything.

03

Views: turning a 3D part into flat pictures

A drawing shows the part as if you looked straight at each face, one at a time. These are orthographic views. Each one shows only two dimensions: the front view shows width and height, the top view shows width and depth, the side view shows depth and height.

The views are lined up with each other on the sheet. A feature in the front view sits directly below the same feature in the top view, and directly beside it in the side view. That alignment is how you connect them in your head.

Third-angle projection (used in the US and by ASME): imagine the part sitting inside a glass box. You look through each glass wall and trace what you see onto the glass, then unfold the box. The top view ends up above the front view, and the right side view ends up to the right.

First-angle projection (Europe and much of Asia, ISO) places them the opposite way: top view below, right view on the left. The cone symbol in the title block tells you which one you're looking at. Mixing them up means you build a mirrored part.

Worked example: the L-block

  • In the front view you see the L shape: the base is 60 wide, the upright is 45 tall.
  • In the top view you see a rectangle 60 × 30 with a line at 15 from the left. That line is the step where the upright meets the base.
  • In the right view you see a rectangle 30 × 45 with a line at 15 up. Looking from the right, you see the end of the base below that line and the upright's face above it.

How many views do you need?

As few as fully describe the part. A flat plate often needs two. A turned shaft usually needs one, because the Ø symbol already tells you it's round. Complex castings may need six views plus sections.

05

Dimensions and the symbols on them

A dimension is built from thin lines. Extension lines come off the part (with a small gap so they don't look like part edges). A dimension line with arrowheads spans between them, and the value sits on it. A leader is a single line with an arrow that points to a feature, used for callouts like hole sizes.

ASME drawings use unidirectional dimensioning: every number reads from the bottom of the sheet, even on vertical dimension lines.

Why not make everything tight?

Tighter tolerances cost more, and the cost climbs steeply. As a rough guide: ±0.5 mm is easy on any machine, ±0.1 is routine CNC work, ±0.02 needs careful setups and good machines, and below ±0.01 you're usually grinding or honing. Make features tight only where function needs it, like a bearing bore, and leave everything else loose.

A 25 ±0.1 shaft. Any diameter in the green band passes. Each of these four measurements is checked against it.

Tolerance stack-up: three 10 ±0.1 blocks stacked end to end. In the worst case, all three are at their limit together, so the stack is 30 ±0.3.

Stack-up example

A spacer (10 ±0.1) sits between a bearing (12 ±0.05) and a nut face, inside a housing pocket 22.5 ±0.1 deep. Worst-case gap = pocket − parts. Largest gap: 22.6 − 9.9 − 11.95 = 0.75. Smallest gap: 22.4 − 10.1 − 12.05 = 0.25. If your design needs at least 0.3 of clearance, this stack-up fails and you need to tighten something.

07

Why plus/minus isn't enough

Plus/minus tolerancing works well for sizes. It works badly for locations and shapes. Here are the three problems GD&T was invented to fix.

Problem 1: square zones. Locate a hole 20 ±0.1 from the left edge and 15 ±0.1 from the bottom. The hole's center can be anywhere in a 0.2 × 0.2 square. But a bolt doesn't care which direction the hole is off. It cares how far. The corners of that square are 0.141 from center and they pass, while a hole only 0.11 off straight to the right fails. That makes no functional sense.

GD&T's position tolerance uses a round zone instead. A Ø0.283 circle passes through the square's corners and gives 57% more usable area (0.0628 mm² versus 0.04 mm²) with no loss of function. More good parts, same assembly.

Problem 2: measured from where? Real parts aren't perfectly square. If the left edge leans, "20 from the left edge" gives a different number at the top than at the bottom. Two inspectors get two answers. GD&T fixes this with datums (lesson 09): you declare exactly which surfaces to measure from and in what order.

Problem 3: shape isn't controlled. A ±0.1 thickness says nothing directly about whether a sealing face is flat, a bore is round, or a hole is square to its face. GD&T has a specific symbol for each of those.

In one sentence

GD&T is a symbolic language that says where to measure from (datums), how shape and orientation are controlled (geometric tolerances), and how much variation each one gets, all in a form every inspector interprets the same way.

08

Features, features of size, MMC and LMC

A feature is any physical surface on a part: a face, a hole wall, a chamfer.

A feature of size is a feature that has a size you can measure between opposing points: holes, pins, shafts, slots, tabs, the thickness of a plate. A flat face on its own isn't a feature of size, because there's nothing opposite it to measure to.

Every feature of size has two extremes:

  • MMC (maximum material condition): the size where the part has the most material. Largest pin, smallest hole. The part is at its heaviest.
  • LMC (least material condition): the size where the part has the least material. Smallest pin, largest hole. The part is at its lightest.

Memory trick

MMC = the heaviest version of the part. Ask "which size leaves more metal?" and you'll never get it backwards.

Why it matters

Assembly is hardest at MMC: the biggest pin going into the smallest hole. That's the worst case for fit. When a hole is bigger than its MMC size, it has extra room, so its location can be a little worse and the bolt still fits. That extra allowance is bonus tolerance, and the Bonus calc tab lets you play with it.

Rule #1 (the envelope principle)

Unless stated otherwise, a feature of size must not break a boundary of perfect form at MMC. A Ø10.0–10.2 pin that measures 10.2 everywhere must be perfectly straight. If it measures 10.1, it can bow up to 0.1. Size tolerance automatically limits form.

Solid = MMC (most material), dashed = LMC (least material). For a hole, MMC is the small one. For a pin, it's the big one.

Rule #1 on a Ø10.0–10.2 pin. The dashed envelope is a perfect Ø10.2 cylinder. A pin at Ø10.1 can bow 0.1 and still fit inside it.

09

Datums: where you measure from

A datum is a theoretically perfect plane, axis, or point that measurements start from. The real surface on the part that establishes it is the datum feature. On the drawing, it's marked with a datum feature symbol: a filled triangle on the surface, connected to a box with a letter.

In the inspection room, datums are physical. The part is set onto precise fixtures (a granite surface plate, angle plates, pins) and those fixtures act as the perfect datums. Datums are used in order:

  • Primary (first letter): the part rests on it, touching at a minimum of 3 points. Stops 3 degrees of freedom: up/down and two tilts.
  • Secondary: the part is pushed against it, touching at 2 points. Stops 2 more: one slide and one spin.
  • Tertiary: the part slides along until it touches at 1 point. Stops the last slide.

This 3-2-1 setup locks all 6 degrees of freedom. Together, the three datum planes are the datum reference frame.

How designers choose datums

Pick the surfaces that locate the part in the real assembly, in the order they make contact.

  • Shock mount plate: the back face bolts flat against the frame tab, so it's A. Then one edge locates against a lip, so it's B. Another edge is C.
  • Shaft: it spins in two bearings, so the two bearing journals are the datums. Together they form one axis, called A-B.
  • Gearbox cover: the gasket face is A, and two dowel pin holes are B and C.

Common mistake

Choosing datums because they're easy to measure from, not because they match how the part mounts. Then a part can pass inspection and still not fit, because inspection didn't hold it the way the assembly does.

10

Basic dimensions and true position

A basic dimension is shown in a box. It describes the exact, theoretically perfect location or shape of a feature. It has no tolerance of its own. Instead, the tolerance comes from a GD&T frame attached to that feature.

The exact spot the basic dimensions point to is called true position. The position tolerance then says how far the real feature may wander from that spot.

Worked example

Three holes are located by basic dimensions [20] [40] [40] from datum B. Each has position tolerance Ø0.2 to A, B, C. That means each hole's center must fall inside its own Ø0.2 circle centered on a perfect spot. The spots are 20, 60, and 100 from B, exactly.

Notice the chained basic dimensions don't stack up. With plus/minus, three chained ±0.1 dimensions could put the last hole off by ±0.3. With basic dimensions, every hole gets the same Ø0.2, no matter how many are in the chain.

Common confusion

"The box means it's exact, so the part must be exact." No. The box means the target is exact. The real part varies inside the zone in the feature control frame.

Boxed basic dimensions set exact targets. Green circles (magnified) show each hole's position zone.

11

The feature control frame: GD&T's sentence

Every GD&T requirement is written in a feature control frame: a row of boxes attached to a feature with a leader. Read it left to right like a sentence.

  1. Symbol: which characteristic is controlled (position, flatness, and so on).
  2. Tolerance: the size of the zone. A Ø in front means the zone is a cylinder. No Ø means two parallel planes or lines.
  3. Modifier (optional): M for MMC or L for LMC. No modifier means RFS: the zone doesn't change with feature size.
  4. Datums (if needed): primary, secondary, tertiary, in that order.
